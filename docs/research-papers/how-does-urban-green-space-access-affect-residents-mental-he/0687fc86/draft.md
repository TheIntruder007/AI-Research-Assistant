# How does urban green space access affect residents' mental health?

Urban green space access is widely hypothesized to improve residents' mental health, yet the empirical evidence reveals a complex relationship that depends on specific environmental features and study design. While some studies suggest broad benefits, others indicate that the type of vegetation matters significantly; for instance, tree canopy exposure is associated with lower psychological distress, whereas high grass cover can be linked to higher odds of distress [1]. Furthermore, physiological stress reduction appears to rely heavily on multisensory engagement, particularly olfactory and auditory stimuli from nature, rather than visual exposure alone [2]. Despite these nuances, systematic reviews generally support a positive association between green space and outcomes like mood and attention, though findings for depression, anxiety, and general health remain mixed or inconsistent [3][4]. The synthesis strategy prioritizes distinguishing between different types of green space indicators and evaluating the robustness of study designs to address confounding. This review maps the current evidence base, highlighting where consensus exists regarding specific mechanisms like stress recovery, where results diverge across health outcomes, and how methodological limitations constrain causal inference.

### Absence of Randomized Controlled Trials for Causal Inference

The majority of studies in the corpus rely on cross-sectional designs or observational cohort data, which limits the ability to infer causality. ### Mechanisms: Sensory Modalities and Physiological Pathways

The corpus lacks a comprehensive understanding of the sensory mechanisms driving mental health benefits. Paper [4] finds that olfactory stimuli may be better for stress reduction than visual ones, but this finding is based on a virtual experiment with a small sample (N=154). There is no evidence in the corpus linking these sensory findings to real-world urban environments or explaining the physiological pathways (e.g., cortisol levels) by which specific sensory inputs reduce stress.

*Evidence:* Paper [4] states: 'There is, however, a lack of understanding of the link between physiological mechanisms and qualities of urban green spaces.' It also notes that currently, planners prioritize visual stimuli despite evidence suggesting olfactory stimuli may be superior for stress reduction in virtual settings.

### Interaction of Safety Perceptions and Green Space Types

There is a significant gap in understanding how different types of green space influence feelings of safety and actual crime rates, which are critical mediators of mental health. Paper [2] explicitly identifies this as an area for future research, noting that particular types of green space may help or harm community safety perceptions.

*Evidence:* Paper [2] states: 'For example, particular types and combinations of green space may also help or harm feelings of community safety and perceptions of (or actual) crime rates, with well-known implications for mental health.' ### Generalizability Beyond Urban Contexts

While Paper [5] attempts to address non-urban settings, the corpus is heavily skewed toward urban contexts. There remains a gap in understanding how green space effects vary across the urban-rural spectrum and whether findings from dense cities apply to suburban or rural areas with different population densities.

*Evidence:* Paper [1] notes that 'very few have focused on the urban context.' Paper [5] contributes a US perspective but acknowledges that 'few studies have considered the role of green space in non-urban settings,' suggesting this remains an under-explored area.
