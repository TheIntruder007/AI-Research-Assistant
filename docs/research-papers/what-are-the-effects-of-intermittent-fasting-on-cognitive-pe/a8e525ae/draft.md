## Literature Review

Theoretical frameworks posit that intermittent fasting (IF) induces neurotrophic effects through mechanisms such as the metabolic switch from glucose to ketone bodies and the activation of signaling pathways like AMPK and mTOR [1]. Animal studies provide empirical support for these theoretical claims, demonstrating synaptic adaptations in the hippocampus and enhanced cognitive function following fasting protocols consistent with caloric restriction models [2]. However, human evidence presents a more complex picture regarding short-term cognitive performance. In healthy subjects, current data do not support a clear positive effect of IF on immediate cognitive metrics [3]. This divergence between theoretical optimism and neutral short-term findings in healthy populations suggests that benefits may be context-dependent or require longer durations to manifest.

A critical moderator of IF outcomes appears to be physical activity level, particularly within elderly cohorts. In sedentary individuals observing Ramadan diurnal intermittent fasting, associative learning performance declined significantly compared to pre-fasting baselines [4]. Conversely, physically active elderly participants who maintained training frequency during the same fasting period exhibited significant improvements in executive function, attention, and memory domains [4]. This contrast indicates that lifestyle factors, specifically exercise, may be necessary to unlock or sustain cognitive benefits under IF conditions. While IF shows promise for reducing risk in neurodegenerative diseases and improving symptoms in specific pathologies like epilepsy and Alzheimer's disease [3], the generalizability of these findings to healthy aging populations remains uncertain. Future research must address whether positive effects persist regardless of age or obesity status, as variations in fasting patterns and caloric intake likely determine intervention success [3].
