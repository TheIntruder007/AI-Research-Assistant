## Literature Review

Animal models provide the most robust evidence for intermittent fasting (IF) enhancing cognitive performance. In wild-type mice subjected to alternate-day fasting for over a year, learning and memory were superior to those on a regular diet [1]. Similarly, caloric restriction in these models is associated with reduced beta-amyloid accumulation, a key pathological marker of Alzheimer's disease [2], alongside synaptic adaptations in the hippocampus that correlate with enhanced function [2]. IF also ameliorates cognitive deficits induced by specific stressors; for instance, a 28-day regimen improved behavioral impairment in diabetic mice [3], while a 30-day protocol attenuated memory loss caused by systemic bacterial infection without altering general locomotor activity [4].

## Limitations

The efficacy of dietary interventions on cognitive performance is not uniform across all nutritional strategies. Evidence from the FITKids randomized controlled trial indicates that a physical activity intervention yielded significant improvements in behavioral inhibition and cognitive flexibility among children, alongside measurable increases in attentional resources during task execution [5]. In contrast, within a distinct population evaluating dietary composition, only a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil produced significant gains in frontal cognition composites compared to controls; supplementation with nuts failed to demonstrate a similar effect [6]. These divergent outcomes suggest that the magnitude of cognitive benefit depends critically on the specific modality—whether physical activity or a particular dietary pattern—and the nature of the nutritional supplement provided.
